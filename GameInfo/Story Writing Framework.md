# Story Writing Framework

> **Status (2026-10-18): a plan. No story has been written or changed.** This is the method for writing good, varied stories in this game and for making Claude's drafts better: what your own writing measures like, what the game's current story text measures like, a list of tics to ban, a craft kit, a genre menu, and a pipeline that can be mostly automated. The things marked **[needs you]** are decisions or material only the author can supply.
>
> Companion files: `Start Stories Script.md` (every scene of the ten starts, generated by `tools/probes/StoryDump.java`) and `Start Stories Seasons Plan.md` (what each start needs next). The numbers below come from `tools/story_stats.py`, which can be re-run on any text.

**What is distinctive about your writing** (my reading of the two pieces; correct me where I am wrong):

1. **Wordplay carries the voice.** Puns and double meanings sit inside ordinary sentences: "I'd like a cheap detective, neat." / "that place sounded like rhymes and punishing times" / "his frame came straight out of my sketch pad" / a bar called the Private Eye, a street called Gumshoe Junction. The joke is never announced; it lives in the word choice.
2. **Genre is worn on purpose, with a wink.** The noir is full of stock furniture (dame, doozy, flatfoot, skedaddle, a cigarette, a Fastback) and the narrator knows it ("let me just say, from the first-person view…"). The pastiche is the point.
3. **A narrator who steps out of the frame.** "But now I'll let the narrator do the narrating for me." / "So much for a first-person story when you're stuck in a second person." The point of view is a toy, not a wall.
4. **Sound and rhythm as a story engine.** The walking bass is both description and plot: "His fingers walked on a line, but they danced to his tune" becomes "a stalking presence in my mind… the same, steady melody of that bass walking after me." In Offworld the pickaxes are a song, the sound of "Clink" "Clank" is its own line. Sensory detail is chosen because it will come back as a clue. (The game already has this habit: the three-note tune in the Tamer, the slow sad tune in the Disciple.)
5. **Rules and limits for powers.** The ability is named (Surveillance), has a method (eye contact), a cost (mentally taxing), and a built-in limit (you only watch from inside the dreamer's point of view). Magic is a workplace with procedures: the "dream world box" with a time limit, "dream duties", a hub, a book called *On the Origin of Dreams* on the radio.
6. **Banter between friends, with teasing.** Stiven: "Is she cute?" / Sebastian: "You know me a little too well I guess." Characters know each other's habits ("You know I like my drinks without ice").
7. **A soft centre under the tough surface.** "I never say no to a crying lady, but that's exactly what got me here." The hero's flaw is stated lightly and costs him something. Hints of a past (a badge buried under a bottle "on that unfateful day") are left on the table.
8. **Riddles and philosophy as character.** The mystery man's question ("You find a book that's the story of your life… the words stop abruptly") shows who he is and who Sebastian is in one exchange. A question that cannot be answered neatly is used as a hook.
9. **Workers and folk fear (Offworld).** Mining crew, a shift whistle, a cough, a wife to get home to, a superstitious man and a sceptic who tease each other, and something old waiting at dusk. The horror is in the routine ("Fully knowing they had to come back again tomorrow"). Names are small and meaningful (Flint, Grim: "Grim's expression matched his name"). Invented terms feel like trade slang (Turquoite, Moon Lung).
10. **Scenes end on a hook or a plain beat, not on a moral.** The detective drives off with the radio cutting out mid-sentence; the miners go home knowing they must come back.

**What not to copy** (these are first-draft habits, which you would likely fix yourself): the repeated "X says / X continues / X responds" tags (62 in 3,683 words), a few slips between past and present tense, and the occasional named emotion ("a look of hopelessness mixed with defeat"). The framework treats these as things to *avoid*, not as voice.

**Too little data to model:** Offworld is 553 words and Postulate is one genre. A voice profile wants 2,000-5,000 words **per voice**, and ideally samples that are not noir or horror. **[needs you]** see section 8.

The scene text is clean and readable, and several scenes are very good (Quill and the cliff, Ember, the Disciple's last cup, Evenfall). But in the aggregate it has a recognizable *machine* shape, and the numbers show it:

| "small / tiny / little" | 129 | 4.2 | 1.6-1.8 |
| "very" | 79 | 2.6 | 0-0.3 |
| Three-item lists ("A, B, and C") | 67 | 2.2 | 0-0.5 |

| "You do not X. You Y." (paired negation) | 21 | 0.7 | 0 |
| "It is not X. It is Y." | 11 | 0.4 | 0 |
| "definitely / probably / exactly" used as a joke | 13 | 0.4 | 0.5 |

Beyond the counts, patterns I can see by reading (not yet counted):

- **Staccato.** Half the sentences are under eight words ("The fox sits down." "The bar sings." "It is definitely the smoke."). That came from the earlier decision to write at a 7th-grade reading level (made for the Palace text, then carried over). It reads clearly and it reads flat: there is no long sentence to build pressure and no short one to release it, because almost everything is short.
- **A closing image on every scene.** Nearly every scene ends on a small, tidy, cozy image or a one-line epiphany: "like a door opening on a warm room", "like a clock in a very small house", "It is for nothing. That is the point." Real stories sometimes end a scene on a mess.
- **Telling the emotion.** "you realise you are crying", "You cry for some time", "your eyes sting from the smoke. It is definitely the smoke." Used once it works. Used in every story it becomes the way a scene closes.
- **One comfort palette.** Kettles, soup, tea, apples, lanterns, a cat, warm rooms, the gentle old creature. It is pleasant and it makes ten different stories feel like one author.
- **Wise old teachers.** Hui, Brannoch, Harl, Wen Jiu, Moss, Zhao, Meng: the story is often delivered by an elder in a flashback or a letter. Letters or notes appear in nine of the ten starts (only the Lucky Fool has none).
- **Little wordplay and no narrator.** Almost none of the humour comes from the language itself (the Lucky Fool, whose comedy comes from situations and a straight-man exchange, is the exception that works).

### 1.3 Why the two you love work (my reading of the scripts)

The Wandering Immortal's Disciple and the Lucky Fool share what the other two lack:

| | Disciple | Lucky Fool | Tamer | Smith |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **One engine, repeated** | three moves, three answers to "what is your name" | a misunderstanding that pays off each time ("That's what a Hidden Master would say") | a mother's tune, three beasts | a father's lessons, a war, a sword |
| **A motif that returns changed** | the tune, the cup, the gourd | the soap, the hat, Pip's newsletter | the tune | the clock "in a very small house" (only at the end) |
| **Someone you spend time with** | the old man exists in every scene as memory, rumour and dream | **Pip is in nearly every scene** and talks back | the beasts appear **once each** at the end of a trip | **Pia is in six of eight chapters but speaks about ten lines**; her past is never told |
| **A want the reader can name** | to find him | to be left alone / to keep his friends | to find the mother | none is named; Pia has none |
| **Time between beats** | small | small | years | **5-10 years of game time between emotional beats** (see below) |
| **A voice** | wry, tender, understated | comic, deadpan | earnest, pastoral | earnest, soft |

And the game's own long-run data (`Long Run Results.md`, T4) shows why the Smith and the Tamer cannot build feeling at all, whatever their prose: the Smith's five counted tasks add up to **215 items at 4-6 items a year**, so the emotional scene after "make 25 items" arrives about **5 years** after the one before it, and none of the three test runs finished (they died at 16, 25, 34 with Evenfall still unmade). The Tamer's three beasts each meet the player for **one scene** and then become an entry in the companion list. You cannot be attached to someone you only met once, and you cannot be moved by a scene whose setup you forgot five years ago.

## 2. The craft kit: how to make a reader care

A reusable checklist for any scene or character. The design question is "what would make someone feel *this*", and the answers repeat.

### 2.1 Seven ingredients of attachment

(Research: companion design in games, summarised in section 9: distinct characters, growth, meaningful interaction, difficult choices, and mechanics that agree with the story.)

1. **A specific, odd, true detail.** Not "a kind old man": a man who tests iron by listening to it, who hums three notes and stops when asked. One strange concrete fact does more than three adjectives. *Rule: every named character gets one detail that nobody else in the game has.*
2. **A small want, named early.** Pia wants to be useful so nobody sends her away; Moss wants to see the sea before he sleeps. A want makes a character act, and makes the reader hope.
3. **Time spent together.** Attachment needs repetition, not a good introduction. A character should appear in at least three scenes spaced out, doing different things, before anything bad happens to them.
4. **Reciprocity.** The character does something *for the player* that they did not have to (leaves a bowl of soup on the anvil, takes the fall for a mistake) and the player can do something for them.
5. **Cost.** A choice that loses something real (Smith: the order for 2,000 stones vs. the soup; Tamer: step off the cliff vs. climb down). Costless choices are decoration.
6. **Risk and loss, honestly kept.** If a beast can die in the game, the story must notice when it does. If nothing is ever at risk, nothing is ever safe.
7. **Callbacks.** An object, phrase or sound from scene 1 returns in scene 8 meaning something else. (The Disciple's cup, mended with gold.) Plan these on the beat sheet; do not discover them.

### 2.2 The game can do what prose cannot: mechanical echo

The story should *notice what the player did*. Available hooks in the code today: what the player crafts (`itemsCrafted`, item type and level), which companions are alive and in the party (`Taming.peek`, `Taming.legend`), friends and relationships, kills, tournaments won, shops/city/sect founded, gifts given (`Gifts`), the Rankings, deaths (the epilogues already read `dead.<person>`), seasons and festivals, the Palace's state. Each story should name three or four of these as *its* mechanical echoes before drafting.

### 2.3 Scene craft (rules of thumb)

- **Enter late, leave early.** Start in the middle of the action and stop before the explanation.
- **One image per scene, chosen because it will return.** Not three similes.
- **Subtext over statement.** Characters talk about the hoe; they mean the father. If a character says exactly what they feel, rewrite it as what they do.
- **Vary the ending.** At most one scene in four may end on a calm, tidy image. Others end mid-gesture, on a line of dialogue, on a decision, on a joke, or on a small wrong note.
- **Humor and grief in the same breath.** Your own pieces do it (the detective's jokes about a crying lady; the miners' bleak routine). The best scenes in the game do it too (the Disciple's "He gave it very late, when he thought you were asleep").
- **Let the reader be ahead of the character, sometimes.** Dramatic irony (we know Pia is lying; the Lucky Fool does not know he is lucky) is cheaper and stronger than a plot twist.
- **Respect the length of a scene.** In a pop-up window the unit is about 150-350 words. Anything longer must be broken by a choice or a change of speaker.

## 3. The voice bible (what Claude is fed before every scene)

A voice is a **card**, written once, kept in the repo, and pasted into the prompt for every writing task. Format of one card:

```
VOICE: <name>
FOR: <which stories / seasons>
SENTENCE: mean N words, mix: X% under 8, Y% over 25; a long sentence builds, a short one lands
DIALOGUE: share, tag habits (a speech tag at most every Nth line; "said" not "responded"), what each speaker's speech is like
IMAGERY: sources the images come from (trade, weather, food), what is never used
DEVICES: wordplay / narrator asides / ellipses / lists / riddles / onomatopoeia - and how often
SCENE SHAPE: how a scene opens, how it ends
NEVER: the voice-specific bans (on top of the global list in section 4)
SAMPLE: 2-3 passages (human-written) that are the target
```

Initial cards, to be filled from your samples **[needs you]**:

| Card | Built from | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **A. The Author's Voice** (wordplay, winking genre pastiche, meta narrator, banter, soft centre) | Postulate | Lucky Fool, Rich Scion, Ghost-Touched casework, any comic or noir season |
| **B. The Working-Class Dread** (routine, onomatopoeia, folk superstition, understatement, ellipses in speech) | Offworld Side Stories | Ghost-Touched, Smith, Silver Crane (a clan as labour and loss), the Palace's "Wicks" |
| **C. The Plain Pastoral** (the current game voice, but with longer sentences and fewer cozy images) | the Disciple and Tamer scripts, and *edited* by you | Tamer, Orphan, Disciple |
| **D. The Court Voice** (dry, ironic, long periodic sentences, understatement; for politics and trade) | needs a sample | Scion, Heir (the Magistrate), Disgraced |
| **E. The Fable Voice** (short, oral, rhythmic, "once there was") | needs a sample | road tales, lessons, little stories |

A "voice extraction prompt" (the suggestion you found) is run once per card: ask for the sentence rhythm, vocabulary and device inventory of the sample **with counts**, then a list of **what the writer never does**. Section 1.1 is that analysis for cards A and B. The most useful part of any profile, according to the practitioners I found, is the list of what the writer *rejects*, and your samples cannot show that on their own: see the reject log in section 6.

## 4. Banned and watched patterns

The research agrees on two things (see section 9): **detection should be mechanical** (a script, not "does this sound like AI?") and **banning a phrase invites its nearest synonym**, so the rule must target the *construction* and say what to do instead.

### 4.1 Hard bans (a lint script fails the draft)

Prose-level:
- "tapestry", "testament to", "beacon of", "little did they know", "a symphony of", "dance of", "the air was thick with", "a wave of [emotion] washed over", "sent shivers down", "couldn't help but", "let out a breath they didn't know they were holding", "ministrations", "orbs".
- Stock endings and wrap-ups: "and in that moment", "and nothing would ever be the same", "some things are worth…" (a moral summary of the scene).
- The em dash as a connector (this project already does not use it; keep it that way).

Construction-level (regex over the draft):
- "It is not X. It is Y." / "Not X, but Y." binary reframes (11 in the game now; allow zero in new text unless the author asks).
- "You do not X. You Y." paired negations (21 now).
- Three-item lists as a rhythm habit (67 now): allowed at most once per scene, and only if the third item is a surprise.
- "[adverb]ly" before a speech verb ("said softly").
- "for a long time", "for some time", "a long while" (24 now): give the actual duration or cut.

### 4.2 Watchlist (counted per 1,000 words; a draft over the limit is rewritten)

| Word / pattern | Limit per 1,000 | Now |
|---|---|---|
| small, tiny, little | 2.0 | 4.2 |
| very, really, quite | 1.0 | 2.6 |
| quiet, quietly, soft, gentle | 0.5 | 1.0+ |
| warm, warmth, cozy | 0.3 | 0.8 |
| "like a [domestic object]" similes | 1 per 500 words | |
| named emotions ("sad", "afraid", "happy", "angry", "grief") | 1 per scene | not yet counted |
| scenes ending with a sentence under 8 words | 1 scene in 3 | not yet counted |
| scenes that end on a calm closing image | 1 in 4 | not yet counted |
| sentences under 8 words | 30% | 51% |

### 4.3 Positive rules (always paired with a ban)

- *Instead of naming the feeling, give the body doing something small and wrong* ("He read the casualty list twice, as if the second reading might change it": the game already has this one). Never "his chest tightened" more than once in a story.
- *Instead of an aphorism, give a consequence.* The scene ends with what someone does next.
- *Instead of a list of three, pick the one detail nobody expects.*
- *Instead of "very small", give the size by comparison to something particular* (a fox "with ears too big for its head").
- *Instead of the wise old mentor explaining, show the mentor getting something wrong.*
- *Instead of a letter, try a ledger entry, a shopping list, an overheard argument, a thing left half-done.* (Letters are overused: nine of ten starts use one.)

Why each rule has a reason attached: Anthropic's prompting guide says explaining *why* helps the model generalise, and that examples steer tone better than adjectives. Every rule in the final lint file carries a one-line reason and, where possible, a before/after.

## 5. A menu of genres and tones (to vary the stories)

Nine of ten start stories currently sit in one register: gentle, earnest, wistful. The Lucky Fool is the only comedy. Variety does not need new plots, only a declared *mode* for each season. Each mode below promises the reader something different:

| Mode | The promise | Signature devices | Where it could go |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Comedy of errors** | Competence is accidental; things go right for the wrong reasons | straight man and fool, running gag that escalates, bureaucracy, mistaken identity | Lucky Fool S1-S3, a Scion trade farce |
| **Hard-boiled pastiche** | Every sentence is a little too cool and the narrator knows it | similes, puns, "dames", a case file, a narrator who talks to the reader | Ghost-Touched casework, a Disgraced Disciple "who did it" |
| **Labour horror / folk dread** | Ordinary work, something old below it | routines, onomatopoeia, superstition vs. scepticism, understatement | Smith S2 (the mine and the forge), Ghost-Touched S2, Silver Crane S2 (the vein) |
| **Pastoral elegy** | Beautiful, patient, sad | seasons, animals, silence, small repeated actions | Tamer, Orphan, Disciple |
| **Caper / heist** | We have a plan; it goes wrong; we win anyway | a crew with specialties, a plan told twice, a twist | Scion S2, Heir S2 (steal the formula back) |
| **Whodunit with fair clues** | The reader could have guessed | the clue is shown in scene 2 and used in scene 7, red herrings that are people | Scion S1 (already), Disgraced |
| **Court intrigue** | Every polite sentence is a threat | rank, gifts, dinners, the unsaid | Heir (Orrin), Scion, Silver Crane S2 |
| **Epistolary** | We read what they did not say aloud | letters, ledgers, notes in margins, a report that omits the key fact | any "found documents" chapter |
| **Fable / folktale** | A rule is taught by a story with a twist | threes, a trickster, a bargain, a moral that is slightly wrong | road tales, lessons, Disciple |
| **Slow-burn romance** | They want each other and will not say it | proximity, objects exchanged, a joke that carries a weight | Orphan (Sol), Smith (Pia), Disgraced (Mei) |
| **War drama** | What the cause costs the people who carry it | a ground-level view, small tasks in a large event, an unnamed enemy | Smith, Silver Crane, a Disciple S3 |
| **Mystery of a person** | Who was he really? | a trail of coins, names, rumours that disagree | Disciple (already), Orphan S2 |
| **Cosmic / metaphysical** | The world is smaller or stranger than it looks | clerical errors, doors, recycling, the Seam | every S3 (see the Palace) |

**Rule:** each season of each story declares one primary mode and one secondary, so the ten starts do not all sound alike and a season can change mode (Heir S1 revenge mystery, S2 caper, S3 metaphysical).

## 6. The creativity engine (how to avoid "on-distribution" output)

Anthropic's own guide says models "converge toward generic, on distribution outputs" unless told to make distinctive choices, and shows a prompt for it (for front-end design, but the principle is the same). For fiction, the same tool is *forced divergence*:

1. **Premise card before any prose.** One page: mode, want, obstacle, cost, motif, mechanical echo, ending image. Written and approved before drafting.
2. **Three bad ideas first.** For each key beat, Claude lists the three most obvious versions, then writes a fourth that avoids all three.
3. **Specificity quota.** Each scene must contain at least two details that could appear in no other scene of the game (a named tool, a local custom, a brand of tea, a rumour) and one sensory detail that is not sight.
4. **Constraint draws.** For a stuck scene, deal random constraints from a deck: "the scene happens during a meal", "one character lies about a number", "no one uses the word X", "the setting is a thing being repaired", "end on a question". Constraints beat "be creative".
5. **Inversion pass.** After the draft: "what would the opposite emotional move be here?" Keep it if it is better.
6. **Voice swap test.** Remove the names from a dialogue. If two speakers cannot be told apart, rewrite one.
7. **Texture banks.** Per region and trade: foods, tools, weather, slang, holidays, a rumour. The writer draws from the bank instead of the default.
8. **Candidate openings.** Five first paragraphs in five voices; the author picks one. (Cheap, and it exposes the author's taste, which feeds the reject log.)
9. **The reject log.** A growing file of *edits you made* to drafts (what you deleted, what you rewrote and how). Practitioners report that a voice lives in the deletions, which a sample alone cannot show. After each batch, you mark up two or three pages; the log updates the bans and the cards.

## 7. The pipeline, and what can be automated

### 7.1 Stages

| # | Stage | Output | Who | Automatable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Inputs | voice card(s), ban list, texture bank, example bank | author + Claude (once) | files |
| 1 | Premise card | one page per story/season | Claude drafts, **author approves** | template |
| 2 | Beat sheet | scenes with trigger, task, mechanical echo, callbacks, mode, target length | Claude drafts, author approves | template |
| 3 | Draft one scene | text in the `Tale*` data format | Claude, **one scene per call**, with the voice card in view | skill |
| 4 | Mechanical lint | list of tics with line numbers | script | **fully** |
| 5 | Critique | a rubric-based review (below) | Claude, in a separate pass | skill |
| 6 | Revise | new draft; re-lint; repeat once | Claude | skill + script |
| 7 | Author read | marks, rejects, rewrites | **author** | no |
| 8 | Log and test | reject log updated; `StoryDump`, `TalesProbe` | Claude | script |

**Why one scene per call:** long outputs drift toward the average; the practitioner sources and the multi-agent critique paper both point to short passes with feedback in between.

**Critique rubric** (specific, not "is it good?"): (1) What is the emotional claim of this scene, and what *evidence* on the page supports it? (2) Which sentence could be in any story? Quote it. (3) Where is a feeling named instead of shown? (4) Which character speaks in a voice that is not theirs? (5) Does the ending state a moral? (6) What would the scene lose if cut? (7) Which callback does it set up or pay off? The critique must quote lines; a review that does not quote is rejected.

### 7.2 Automation options (cheapest first)

| Option | What it does | Cost | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. A `StoryLint` tool** (`tools/story_stats.py` is its seed, or a Java probe next to `StoryDump`) | Runs the section-4 bans and counts on any file or on all `Tale*.java` text; prints the lines at fault and the densities | small | the key piece: mechanical, repeatable, also usable on the existing ten stories |
| **B. A project skill `/story`** (`.claude/skills/story/SKILL.md` with `voices.md`, `bans.md`, `rubric.md`, `examples.md`, `scripts/lint.py`) | Loads the voice cards and rules only when a story task starts; steps 3-6 as a checklist; runs the lint itself | small | skills load on demand and can bundle scripts; the project already uses scratch scripts of this kind |
| **C. A hook** (`PostToolUse` on edits to `Tale*.java` or `GameInfo/*Story*.md`) | Runs the lint after every write and feeds failures back to Claude to fix before it reports done | small | exit code 2 sends the message back to Claude; the project's compile rule is already a similar habit |
| **D. CLAUDE.md / memory rules** | A short standing rule: "story text follows `Story Writing Framework.md`; never write a scene without the voice card" | tiny | the full rules stay out of CLAUDE.md to keep it short |
| **E. Reject log + example bank** (`GameInfo/story-bank/`) | Your edits and your best passages, pasted into the prompt (3-5 diverse examples work best; they must vary so the model does not copy one) | ongoing | the most valuable input and the only one only you can make |
| **F. A second reader** | A fresh-context critique, or you as the reader | | the multi-agent paper's gain came from independent reviewers; you have asked for no sub-agents, so the second reader is *you*, via the "choose the best of five openings" and "mark up two pages" steps |

**Recommended first build** (when you say go): A + B + D, with the voice cards A and B and the ban list as written here, run on **one** existing scene (the Smith's "A Cold Forge") as the pilot: lint it, rewrite it in a chosen voice, show you the before/after.

### 7.3 Measuring success
No reading-level target (the plain-voice experiment shows the cost). Instead: tic densities under the limits; sentence-length spread (standard deviation up, share under 8 words down to about 30%); each scene's ending type varied; and the only test that matters: **would the author read it aloud to a friend**.

## 8. Needed from the author

1. **More of your writing**, about 2,000-5,000 words per voice, ideally a *different* genre from noir (a comic scene, a sad scene, a long descriptive passage, dialogue-only). Even rough drafts help. Postulate's first-person sections and third-person sections should be marked if the switch is deliberate.
2. **Three or four passages you love by other authors** (any genre) and, for each, one line on why. These are the targets Claude cannot guess.
3. **Edits.** Two or three pages of existing game text with your cuts and rewrites marked (the Smith's "A Cold Forge" and the Tamer's "Old Ember" would be good).
4. **Deal-breakers**: words, jokes, topics, kinds of ending you never want.
5. **Voice per story**: which cards (section 3) fit which starts. My suggestions are in the Seasons Plan.
6. Is **wordplay** something you want in the game's main text, or only in comic modes?

- Research: [LLM Review: creative writing via blind peer review feedback](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.08003) (independent reviewers keep drafts from converging); [Art or Artifice? LLMs and the false promise of creativity](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.14556) (professional writers on cliché in model fiction); [prompt sophistication and story quality](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03746v1).
- Anthropic: [Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices) (3-5 diverse examples, explain why, "converge toward generic, on distribution outputs" and the instruction to avoid it); Claude Code [skills](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/skills) and [hooks](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks-guide).
- Game narrative and companion attachment: [exploring the design of companions in video games](https://www.universityxp.com/research/2021/12/2/exploring-the-design-of-companions-in-video-games), [advanced game narrative design techniques](https://www.wayline.io/blog/advanced-game-narrative-design-techniques), [Project Eternity and characterization](https://www.gamebanshee.com/kahp) (Avellone: start from mechanics, "why would a player bring this companion along?"), a Finnish thesis on designed emotional connection (distinct and responsive characters; attachment through growth; difficult choices).

*Caveat:* none of the sources tested these rules on long-form fiction with a model of this generation. The rules are starting points; the lint and the reject log are how they get tuned to you.
